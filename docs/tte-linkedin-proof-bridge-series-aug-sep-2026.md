## B01 — 14 Aug 2026 — Founder POV

A lot of ecommerce brands do not need another campaign first.

They need to understand what happens after someone shows intent.

They browse.
They add to cart.
They reach checkout.
They buy once.
Then what?

Every one of those moments can either become part of a system or be left to chance.

That is the part of email marketing I am most interested in.

Not sending more for the sake of activity.

Building a customer journey that notices intent and responds properly.

The more I work on 222 Emails, the more I think the real question is not “What should we send this week?”

It is:

**Where is good buying intent currently being wasted?**

That question usually leads somewhere useful.

## B02 — 16 Aug 2026 — Opinion

If an ecommerce brand spends heavily to acquire customers but barely follows them up after purchase, the acquisition strategy is incomplete.

The first order is not the finish line.

It is the point where the economics can start improving.

A customer who has already bought is easier to understand than a stranger.

You know what they chose.
You know when they bought.
You know what may logically come next.

That creates opportunities for education, cross-sell, replenishment, repeat purchase and retention.

I think more brands should judge acquisition by what happens **after** the first transaction, not just the ROAS that created it.

## B03 — 18 Aug 2026 — Diagnostic

Three questions I would ask before telling an ecommerce brand it needs more traffic:

1. What percentage of new subscribers ever receive a proper buying journey?
2. What happens when someone reaches cart or checkout and leaves?
3. What happens after purchase #1?

If those three areas are weak, more traffic may simply feed a leaky system faster.

Traffic matters.

But conversion, recovery and retention determine how much value the business actually extracts from it.

I would rather understand the leaks first.

## B04 — 21 Aug 2026 — Founder learning

One thing building 222 Emails has made me appreciate more:

The best automation often feels less automated to the customer.

A replenishment reminder at the right time feels useful.

A post-purchase tip feels like service continuing.

A cart reminder can feel helpful when it removes friction instead of shouting “BUY NOW”.

The technology is not the interesting part.

The interesting part is using timing, behaviour and context well enough that the message makes sense when it arrives.

That is the standard I want our lifecycle systems held to.

## B06 — 27 Aug 2026 — Myth busting

A welcome flow is not “the email with the discount”.

It is the first structured sales journey a new subscriber experiences.

The discount might help.

But the real work is broader:

Who are you?
Why should I trust you?
What should I buy first?
Why is it relevant to me?
What uncertainty is stopping me?
Why should I act now?

Answer those questions well and the welcome flow becomes far more useful than a coupon delivery mechanism.

## B08 — 2 Sep 2026 — Teardown framework

When I look at an ecommerce customer journey, I mentally split it into four buckets:

**Convert**
Welcome and browse.

**Recover**
Cart and checkout.

**Grow**
Post-purchase, cross-sell and replenishment.

**Retain**
Repeat purchase, win-back and VIP.

That simple framework makes it much easier to spot where the business is over-reliant on acquisition.

If one bucket is nearly empty, there is usually a lifecycle opportunity hiding there.

## B09 — 5 Sep 2026 — Customer value

A customer buying twice can matter more than it looks.

Purchase #1 proves they were willing to trust the brand.

Purchase #2 proves the relationship can repeat.

That second order is why I pay so much attention to post-purchase, replenishment and retention systems.

The question is not only:

“How do we get more customers?”

It is also:

“How do we make more of the customers we already earned worth keeping?”

Those two questions should live in the same growth strategy.

## B11 — 11 Sep 2026 — Anti-discount POV

Not every lifecycle problem needs a discount.

Sometimes the customer needs reassurance.

Sometimes they need product education.

Sometimes delivery information is unclear.

Sometimes the timing is simply wrong.

Sometimes they forgot.

Discounting can work, but it should not become the default answer to every hesitation.

A stronger system diagnoses the reason for the drop-off and uses the right response for that stage of the journey.
